Walk into a fabric market in Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer) and what catches the eye first is usually wax print (often called pagne wax in French), with its saturated colours and bold patterns. Many people assume it is a "traditional African fabric", but its story spans Java, the Netherlands and the West African coast — a tale of intercontinental trade and design. Knowing that history helps you understand how to look at a piece of cloth.

## From Java to the Netherlands: a technique industrialised

The Asian Textile Studies website explains that batik, a wax-resist dyeing technique, is Javanese. In the mid-nineteenth century, Dutch and other European manufacturers began imitating it, intending to sell it back to what was then the Dutch East Indies. But local people generally considered the imported cloth inferior to hand-made batik, and with high tariffs on top, sales were poor, so the manufacturers turned to other markets.

A case study from the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) notes that the forerunner of today's Vlisco group was founded in Helmond, the Netherlands, in 1846. Material from a 2025 exhibition jointly organised by the HEAR art school in France and the Musée de l'Impression sur Etoffes in Mulhouse sets out the key dates: in the 1890s, the Scottish merchant Ebenezer Brown Fleming began promoting wax print in African markets, and the first consignment of cloth arrived in the Gold Coast (today's Ghana) in 1895.

A story you often hear is that West African soldiers returning home after service in Indonesia brought batik back with them and sparked the fashion. However, a 2019 study by van Kessel, published by the African Studies Centre Leiden, finds no evidence that these veterans played any role in introducing a new fashion. The story is widely told, but it is best treated as an "unverified legend".

## West Africa made it "African cloth"

What truly rooted wax print in West Africa was local markets and traders. The exhibition material notes that from the 1950s, women cloth traders in Togo began naming the patterns they distributed exclusively; their businesses did so well that they became known as the "Nana Benz" (because they could afford Mercedes-Benz cars). Manufacturers gave designs only a number; the names were chosen by African sellers. As a result, the same cloth might carry names full of everyday wisdom, such as "Rival's Eye" or "Money Flies".

## Today's market: who makes the cloth

According to the same exhibition material, Asian manufacturers now account for 96% of the wax print market; Vlisco is the only European producer still operating, and Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana are among the few African locations still producing wax print after most factories closed.

When admiring or buying, a few things are worth noticing: genuine wax-resist cloth often shows irregular "crackle" lines and colour variations, so every metre is slightly different. And ask the seller what the pattern is called — you will often hear a story.

## One line for Asian readers

Wax print is a "mixed-heritage" cloth: the technique came from Asia, the industrialisation from Europe, but its life was given by West Africa. Listen to a pattern's name before you wear it, and you will better understand why it belongs to Africa.
