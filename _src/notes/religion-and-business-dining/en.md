In Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer), it is perfectly ordinary for Muslims, Catholics and Protestants to share the same business table. Religious diversity also means that hosting calls for a little extra thought: what is served, what is poured and when the meeting is scheduled can all affect whether your guests feel at ease accepting.

## Religious demographics

According to 2021 census data cited by the U.S. Department of State:

| Group | Share of total population |
|---|---|
| Islam | 42.5% |
| Christianity (combined) | 39.8% (of which Catholics about 17%) |
| No religion | 12.6% |

The great majority of Muslims are Sunni, many of them influenced by Sufi traditions. Geographically, Muslims predominate in the north and Christians in the south, but followers of all the major faiths live throughout the country, and Abidjan has a large Muslim population.

Only a small share of people describe themselves as followers of traditional (animist) religions in the statistics, yet many Christians and Muslims retain elements of traditional belief in daily life, and families with both Muslim and Christian members are common. So do not guess someone's religion from their name, place of origin or appearance; when it matters, simply ask politely about dietary preferences.

## Food: ask first, then book

When inviting Muslim guests, the usual dietary considerations include:

- **Pork and pork products**: including lard and gelatine made from pork or non-halal sources (often found in desserts and gummy sweets).
- **Alcohol**: not only drinks, but also dishes cooked with wine or spirits.
- **Origin of meat**: some people eat only halal meat slaughtered according to Islamic rules; fish with scales is generally not an issue.

Observance varies from person to person: some are relaxed, others very strict. The best approach is to ask in advance: "Avez-vous des préférences ou des restrictions alimentaires ?" (Do you have any dietary preferences or restrictions?), and to choose a restaurant whose menu includes fish, chicken and non-alcoholic drinks. If anyone at the table is eating with their hands, remember to use your right hand; when dining with Muslims, avoid passing food or objects with your left hand.

## Timing: Ramadan and religious holidays

**Ramadan.** During Ramadan, Muslims fast from sunrise to sunset; the elderly, the sick and those for whom fasting would be a serious burden are exempt. Ramadan follows the Islamic (lunar) calendar, so its Gregorian dates change every year. Based on astronomical calculations, Ramadan 2027 is expected to begin around 8 February, with Eid al-Fitr around 9 or 10 March; the actual dates depend on the moon sighting.

Tips for scheduling during Ramadan:

- Do not arrange lunches or daytime coffee meetings; if you must meet during the day, avoid eating or drinking in front of your counterpart.
- Consider an invitation to an iftar dinner instead. Sharing a meal after sunset is also a formal mode of diplomatic and business hospitality; the U.S. Ambassador to Côte d'Ivoire, for example, has hosted an iftar at the official residence.
- In the late afternoon, close to sunset, your counterpart may need to leave on time, so do not let meetings overrun.

**Public religious holidays.** Côte d'Ivoire's statutory holidays include both Islamic and Christian festivals: for example Eid al-Fitr, Tabaski (Eid al-Adha), the days after the Night of Destiny and the Prophet's Birthday (Mawlid), as well as Easter Monday, Ascension Day, Assumption Day and Christmas. The dates of Islamic holidays move every year and are sometimes confirmed by the government only a few days beforehand: the holiday of 16 March 2026 (the day after the Night of Destiny), for instance, was announced only on 12 March. Build some flexibility into your schedule.

On ordinary weekdays, business lunches generally take place between 12 noon and 2 pm.

## One line for Asian teams

Asking about dietary needs and checking the calendar before you host shows more respect than any dish on the menu.
