If you had to pick one kind of music that best captures urban life in Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer), zouglou is a great place to start. Born in university halls of residence, with blunt, straight-talking lyrics, it has spent more than thirty years travelling from campus to street-corner bars, becoming a shared soundtrack for many Ivorians.

## 1990: it began with a dance in the halls

According to Music in Africa, zouglou emerged in 1990, initially as a dance: students living in university halls in Abidjan's Yopougon district raised both arms in angular movements, as if appealing to the heavens about the hardships of student life. The students gave the dance a name, which some reports link to a Baoulé expression for "piled up together like odds and ends" — a metaphor for crowded life in the halls.

Bilé Didier, regarded as one of its founders, recalled at a panel during the 2024 Abidjan zouglou festival (Feza) that zouglou originated in the university halls around 1990, and that at the time even its creators never imagined it would become a national cultural identity more than thirty years later.

## Where the music comes from

Zouglou's direct foundation is a singing style called ambiance facile (also known as wôyô): during school sports competitions in the 1980s, student supporters made up their own chants, accompanied by whatever instruments came to hand, such as metal scrapers, glass bottles and drums. Its earlier roots can be traced to styles from western Côte d'Ivoire, such as Tohourou and Aloucou, that were popular in the cities in the 1960s and 1970s.

Because it absorbed rhythms and melodies from all over the country, zouglou is considered the first multi-ethnic music genre able to represent the whole nation. From the start its lyrics were direct, describing student life and everyday situations, often with humorous social observation.

## Landmark works across three generations

| Period | Group/artist | Signature song |
|---|---|---|
| First generation (early 1990s) | Didier Bilé and Les Parents du Campus | "Gboglo Koffi" (1991) |
| | Poignon et Zougloumania | "Zomamanzo" (1991) |
| | Les Potes de la Rue | "Ziopin" (1992) |
| Second generation (from the mid-1990s) | Yodé & Siro | "Asec Kotoko" (1996) |
| | Magic System | "Premier Gaou" (2000) |
| | Les Garagistes | "Tapis Rouge" (2003) |
| Third generation (from the late 2000s) | Zouglou Makers | "Djamo Djamo" (2014) |

In the first generation, Zougloumania took zouglou from campus into Abidjan's working-class neighbourhoods, and Système Gazeur and the singer Lago Paulin were also names of this period. From the mid-1990s, Les Salopards' "Génération Sacrifiée" (1997) and Espoir 2000's "Gloire à Dieu" (2006) were signature songs of the second generation.

Magic System's "Premier Gaou" in particular was a huge hit across Africa and in France, taking zouglou beyond Côte d'Ivoire. In the late 2000s the record industry was hit hard by piracy, and the largest label, Showbiz, closed at the end of 2008; at the same time, live performances in maquis (open-air eateries) grew increasingly popular, becoming zouglou's new stage.

## How to hear it live

Zouglou still has its own festival. From 1 to 3 March 2024, the Abidjan zouglou festival (Feza) was held at the Jesse Jackson sports complex in Yopougon. Launched by the cultural events organiser Aristide Tapé, its line-up included groups from across the generations, such as Les Garagistes, Yodé et Siro and Lago Paulin. If you are in Abidjan, keeping an eye out for festivals like this or for live shows at maquis is the most direct way in.

## One line for Asian readers

To get a feel for the everyday emotions of Côte d'Ivoire, listen to the songs in the table above in chronological order and pay attention to the humour and details of daily life in the lyrics — it will bring you closer to how local people feel than any guided tour.
