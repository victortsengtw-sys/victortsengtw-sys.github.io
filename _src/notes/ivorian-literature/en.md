The French-language literature of Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer) offers a fine window onto a century of social change in the country. Several of its leading writers have won major French literary prizes, and their works are available in English translation, so they are quite accessible to Asian readers. Here are three writers to start with.

## Bernard Binlin Dadié: the father of Ivorian literature

Bernard Dadié was born in Assinie in 1916 and died on 9 March 2019, aged 103. The French publishing trade magazine Livres Hebdo called him "the father of Ivorian literature". His work spans poetry, novels, drama, folk tales and travel writing. He made his mark as early as 1934 with the satirical play *Les Villes*, and in 1950 published the poetry collection *Afrique debout !*. His semi-autobiographical novel *Climbié*, from the 1950s, is regarded as his best-known work, while *Le Pagne noir* is a collection of folk tales subtitled "contes africains" (African tales).

As the titles suggest, he also recorded his observations of other cities, in *Un Nègre à Paris* and *Patron de New York*; the latter, together with *La Ville où nul ne meurt*, earned him the Grand prix littéraire d'Afrique noire twice. In the theatre, he produced *Monsieur Thôgô-Gnini* and *Béatrice du Congo* in 1970. He served as Minister of Culture from 1977 to 1986, and in 2016 received UNESCO's first Jaime Torres Bodet Prize. Today the Palais de la Culture in Abidjan, which hosts the MASA performing arts market, bears his name.

## Ahmadou Kourouma: a novelist launched from Montreal

Ahmadou Kourouma was born in Boundiali in 1927 into a Malinké family and died in Lyon, France, in 2003. His first novel, *Les Soleils des indépendances* (The Suns of Independence), was turned down by several French publishers before winning a literary competition in Montreal; it was published by the Presses de l'Université de Montréal in 1968 and reissued in France in 1970. Its protagonist, Fama, is a descendant of Malinké royalty, and the book won the Grand prix littéraire d'Afrique noire in 1969.

After that, he published a novel only every few years: *Monnè, outrages et défis* (1990), *En attendant le vote des bêtes sauvages* (1998) and *Allah n'est pas obligé* (2000). The last, narrated in the voice of a child soldier, won both the Prix Renaudot and the Goncourt des lycéens that same year, as well as the Prix Jean-Giono. The previous novel, *En attendant le vote des bêtes sauvages*, received the 1999 Prix du Livre Inter. At his death he left an unfinished novel, *Quand on refuse on dit non*, later published by Seuil.

| Véronique Tadjo | *Reine Pokou* (2005) | — |

Véronique Tadjo, born in 1955, is a leading contemporary writer whose work includes poetry, novels and children's books. She won the 2005 Grand prix littéraire d'Afrique noire for *Reine Pokou* and in 2016 received Côte d'Ivoire's national Grand Prix for literature named after Bernard Dadié. Her English-language novel *In the Company of Men* won the Los Angeles Times Book Prize for fiction, and in 2024 the University of St Andrews in Scotland awarded her an honorary Doctor of Letters.

## Finding the books

The French originals of these works are mostly published by French houses such as Seuil, and the English translations can be found through major online bookshops or libraries. As you read, pay attention to the spoken idiom, proverbs and narrative rhythm of the stories, and compare them with the French literature you already know.

## One line for Asian readers

If you read only one book, start with an English translation of Kourouma; to feel the rhythm of Ivorian storytelling, follow up with Dadié's folk tales.
