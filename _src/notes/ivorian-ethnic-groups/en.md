Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer) is home to more than sixty ethnic groups, and newcomers can easily get lost in the long list of names. In practice, scholars and country reference works usually group them into four major cultural clusters. Learn this map first and you will make sense more quickly of the languages, festivals and trading networks you encounter around the country.

## The four cultural clusters at a glance

| Cluster | Main area | Representative groups | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Akan (East Atlantic) | South-east, east of the Bandama River | Baoulé, Agni | Traditionally mostly agricultural, with historically more centralised chiefdoms; descent traditionally traced through the maternal line |
| Kru (West Atlantic) | South-west, west of the Bandama River | Bété | Generally considered the earliest of today's groups to settle in the area; traditionally mostly patrilineal |
| Mandé | North-west | Malinké, Bambara, Dioula (Juula) | Extends into northern Guinea and Mali |
| Gur / Voltaic | North-east and north-central | Sénoufo | Mainly agricultural, with diverse religious beliefs |

In addition, the lagoon area of the south-east is home to a set of peoples collectively known as the "lagoon cultures". They traditionally lived from fishing and farming, generally did not form centralised authorities above village level, and are sometimes listed separately.

As for population shares, a 2016 cultural guide from the US Defense Language Institute states that the Baoulé are the country's largest ethnic group, making up at least 15% of the total population. Figures for other groups mostly date from the 1980s, so they are not given here.

This classification rests mainly on linguistic and cultural kinship. Social organisation also varies within each cluster: among the Sénoufo, for example, small family descent groups coexist with more complex forms of alliance, while Kru peoples were traditionally organised by village and seldom formed centralised chiefdoms. Understanding these differences helps explain why traditional leaders, elders and community decision-making do not work in quite the same way everywhere.

## Why this matters for business

**1. Reading cross-border networks.** The Library of Congress country study notes that, for each of these clusters, more members live outside Côte d'Ivoire than inside it. The Akan, for instance, share historical roots with the Asante kingdom in Ghana to the east, while Mandé peoples extend into northern Guinea and Mali. If your target is the wider West African market, these cultural ties often double as channels for trade and personal connections.

**2. Understanding the language landscape.** French is the official language, but in markets and among traders, Dioula, which is closely related to the Mandé languages, serves as the country's common trade language. It is especially widely used among Muslim merchants. On the streets of Abidjan you will also hear a popular French-based vernacular. If you often work in markets or with partners in the north, learning a few Dioula greetings is one way to show goodwill.

**3. Reading festivals and the local calendar.** The Akan, for example, hold a yam festival to give thanks for the harvest, while the Man region in the west has a mask festival. Knowing the key local festivals before visiting clients or planning events helps you avoid clashes and gives you something to talk about.

## Using this map with care

- **Cultural clusters are broad categories, not identity labels.** Internal migration and inter-ethnic marriage are gradually blurring traditional ethnic boundaries.
- **Ethnicity is not the same as religion, nor the same as region.** A single group may include Muslims, Christians and followers of traditional beliefs.
- **There is no need to ask someone about their ethnicity.** If they bring up their home town or mother tongue themselves, simply listen with curiosity.

## One line for Asian teams

Treat the four cultural clusters as an introductory map for understanding Côte d'Ivoire, not as a basis for judging individuals; real understanding comes from direct exchange with each partner.
