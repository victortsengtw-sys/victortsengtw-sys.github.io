In Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer), Easter is more than a religious holiday. For the Baoulé people of the centre of the country, it has a name of its own: Pâquinou. Every year at this time, many people working in Abidjan head back to their home villages — a little like the Lunar New Year homecoming in Asia. If you work with Ivorian partners, understanding Pâquinou will help you avoid a "dead zone" in your schedule.

## Easter, with a Baoulé flavour

Pâquinou is the Baoulé name for Easter, and it blends the Christian festival with Baoulé traditional customs. The media describe it as a three-day celebration that brings communities back to life, and note that in Côte d'Ivoire the festival now often carries more cultural than religious meaning.

The blend is visible in church, too. A 2025 report by the official news agency AIP described Pâquinou events held by several Catholic parishes in Abidjan: some celebrated an entire Mass in Baoulé, others combined it with traditional dance, food fairs and tributes to traditional chiefs, all to recreate the atmosphere of a village Pâquinou in the city. The Sainte Anne parish in Port-Bouët, for example, held its 13th edition on Easter Monday, with Mass in several local languages, food stalls and folk dancing; other parishes moved the event to a different date and combined it with their parish anniversary. For those who cannot travel home, it is another way to celebrate in the city.

## What happens during the homecoming

Pâquinou is above all about reunion. Typical activities include:

- **Family gatherings**: relatives who have not seen each other for years come together again, strengthening sibling and family bonds.
- **Settling disputes**: with the whole extended family present, it is a chance to resolve community or family disputes.
- **Village development**: many villages use the occasion to bring together members working elsewhere to discuss and advance local development projects.
- **Food and celebration**: local dishes and palm-based drinks are prepared for the festivities.

Reports also note that some people save all year just for this trip home, while those who cannot make it often gather in Abidjan's maquis (open-air eateries) to celebrate together.

## Impact on transport and work

On the eve of Pâquinou, long-distance transport out of Abidjan comes under heavy pressure. In 2026, for example, Easter fell on 5 April; *Fraternité Matin* reported that by Friday 3 April the Adjamé long-distance bus station was already packed, with long ticket queues, taxis hard to find and transport companies laying on extra departures. The main destinations include central towns such as Bouaké, Sakassou, Tiébissou, Dimbokro and Bongouanou. Easter Monday (Lundi de Pâques) is also a public holiday in Côte d'Ivoire.

## One line for Asian companies

When planning second-quarter visits, shipments or events, leave the days around Easter free for local colleagues to go home; confirming your partners' schedules early is far better for the working relationship than discovering at the last minute that "everyone has gone back to the village".
