In Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer), a funeral is rarely a purely private family matter; it is a social event involving the extended family, the village, colleagues and friends. When a business partner or colleague suffers a bereavement, the way you pay your respects will be remembered for a long time. Understanding the meaning and sequence of funerals helps you do the right thing at the right time.

## The social meaning of funerals

Côte d'Ivoire is diverse in religion and ethnicity, and funeral customs vary greatly by region, ethnic group and faith; what follows describes only common patterns.

An article (2025) by a sociologist at Alassane Ouattara University in Bouaké notes that, in traditional thought, death is understood as a passage to another world, and the cemetery connects the living and the dead; in the community studied, funeral costs are considerable and are often borne by family members with a steady income. Local media reports also show that large funerals can draw several hundred people, with relatives, people from the same home town, colleagues and neighbourhood associations contributing cash, fabric, drinks or food — sometimes announced publicly during the wake.

Precisely because so many people are involved, burials can be a long time coming. Former president Henri Konan Bédié, for example, died in August 2023 and was buried in his home town about ten months later, on a date that had to be agreed jointly by the family and the traditional chiefs of the Baoulé community. Ordinary families work to much shorter timelines, but local reports also describe cases where a body remained at the funeral home for about two and a half months before the wake and burial took place.

## Common stages and French vocabulary

Obituaries published in newspapers usually include a *programme des obsèques* (funeral programme) listing the date, time and venue of each stage:

| French | English | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| condoléances | condolences | Condolences are received at the bereaved family's home, possibly in informal and formal phases, over several days to several weeks |
| veillée (funèbre) | wake | Often includes religious and traditional rites, and may go on all night |
| levée du corps | removal of the body | The body is collected from the funeral home, sometimes then taken back to the home village |
| inhumation | burial | Often follows a religious service (such as a requiem Mass) |
| messe d'action de grâce | thanksgiving Mass | Catholic families may hold one after the burial |

Obituaries often use *rappel à Dieu* ("called home to God") as a euphemism for death.

## What foreign partners can do

- **Reach out first**: once you hear the news, express your sympathy as soon as possible by phone, message or card. A formal sentence in French: *Je vous présente mes plus sincères condoléances.* (Please accept my most sincere condolences.)
- **Use *condoléances*, not *sympathies***: the OQLF points out that condolences are expressed with the plural *condoléances*, together with verbs such as *présenter* or *exprimer* — not *souhaiter*, because condolences are not a "wish".
- **Keep written condolences short and sincere**: say how you know the person and mention the qualities of the deceased; you can close with *mes salutations attristées*.
- **Ask before attending**: check with a colleague who knows the family well whether it is appropriate to pay a condolence visit or attend the wake, and when best to go.
- **Allow some flexibility at work**: funeral proceedings can stretch over several weeks and may require travel back to the home village; be understanding if your counterpart is temporarily unavailable or needs time off.

## One line for Asian readers

In Côte d'Ivoire, a bereavement is one of the most remembered moments in any relationship; a timely, well-judged message of condolence carries more weight than any business gift.
