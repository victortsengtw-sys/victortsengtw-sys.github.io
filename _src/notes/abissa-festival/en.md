Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer) has many traditional festivals, and one of the best known is Abissa, held in the historic seaside town of Grand-Bassam. It is the traditional new year of the N'zima people (also called Appolo), and a community ritual of forgiveness, unity and fresh starts. For anyone wanting to understand how local society resolves conflict, Abissa is an excellent way in.

## What is Abissa?

According to the N'zima, Abissa is a dance taught to them by a spirit named Afoantchè, to connect the living with their ancestors and renew the covenant between the people and the spirits; it is also a purification ritual. The N'zima community is made up of seven families, of which the Nvavilé family is regarded as the one that discovered and guards the dance. Each year, Abissa brings the seven families together again around the king and the sacred drum.

The festival falls roughly between late October and early November, but the exact dates vary from year to year: in 2024 it ran from 27 October to 3 November, while in 2025 the public celebrations opened on 12 October. Besides Grand-Bassam, it is also held in Tiapoum and several surrounding villages.

## Three phases

According to *Fraternité Matin*, the core of Abissa falls into roughly three phases:

| Phase | What happens |
|---|---|
| Siedu | About a week before the official start, the sacred drum Edo-n'gbolé enters a period of retreat, during which strict taboos apply — for example, no other drums may be played and no funerals are held |
| Gouazo | Abissa is officially opened to the public: the Nvavilé family hands the sacred drum to the N'zima king, who then hands it to the people, and the celebrations spread through the town |
| Ewudolé | The climax, carnival-like, with parades in full costume, masks and all kinds of disguises |

During the festival, the heads of each family gather to perform the "dance of the chiefs", receiving blessings and praying to the ancestors for peace and prosperity. Every day residents parade in brightly coloured traditional dress; some cover their bodies in white kaolin, and cross-dressing — men dressed as women and women as men — is a common sight.

## "Social criticism" and public reconciliation

What makes Abissa most distinctive is that it preserves a period of free speech. During this time, people can publicly point out problems in the community and grievances against its leaders — through song and other means — and even criticise the king himself, without being punished for it; those criticised are expected to accept the remarks and apologise. People who have committed serious wrongs can also use this moment to repent publicly and ask the community's forgiveness, while outstanding members are publicly honoured.

In other words, every year Abissa gives the community a "settling of accounts": accumulated resentments are spoken aloud and dealt with, and everyone then welcomes the new year together.

## Things to bear in mind as a spectator

Abissa is first and foremost a religious and community ritual, and only secondarily a tourist event. The rites surrounding the sacred drum carry taboos, so it is best to ask a local guide or the organisers before taking photos or approaching ritual sites. The dates are set each year by the traditional authorities, so check that year's announcement before you travel.

## One line for Asian readers

If you are in Abidjan in October or November, it is worth setting aside a day for Abissa in Grand-Bassam; it shows that "public criticism" and "mutual forgiveness" can also be a form of social wisdom that is institutionalised and celebrated.
