Mention Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer) and most people think only of cocoa. Yet in the 1970s the country was the world's third-largest coffee producer, growing almost exclusively robusta. Knowing this origin story helps you understand where West African coffee sits in terms of flavour and its role in the market.

## From the colonial era to the 1970s peak

Coffee was introduced to Côte d'Ivoire shortly after French colonisation. At first only small quantities of Liberian coffee (*Coffea liberica*) were grown; robusta was brought in from Java and the Congo in the early 20th century and gradually became the main variety.

Production grew rapidly after the Second World War, from about 36,000 tonnes in 1945 to around 112,500 tonnes by 1958. Expansion continued after independence in 1960, and in the 1970s the country ranked third in the world, behind only Brazil and Colombia; output in 1970 was about 280,000 tonnes, making it also Africa's largest coffee producer. Exports at the time often exceeded 300,000 tonnes.

## Ups and downs since 2000

Robusta production peaked at about 380,000 tonnes in 2000, then fell sharply during two periods of domestic unrest between 2002 and 2011, dropping to about 32,000 tonnes in 2011.

Output has remained volatile in recent years. According to official figures cited by the Ivorian government's economic information website:

| Year | Output (thousand tonnes) |
|---|---|
| 2015 | 126.1 |
| 2019 | 95.2 |
| 2022 | 95.5 |
| 2023 | 46.9 |
| 2024 | 72.2 (provisional) |

The official analysis cites irregular rainfall during flowering, a shortage of improved planting material, farmers' financial difficulties and ageing coffee trees. Coffee also follows a natural cycle of "on" and "off" years, with a bumper harvest often followed by a smaller one. The whole coffee and cocoa sector is now regulated by the Conseil du Café-Cacao (Coffee-Cocoa Council).

## Growing regions, varieties and processing

Robusta is mostly grown at altitudes of around 300 to 400 metres, in regions including Abengourou in the east, Daloa and Gagnoa in the centre-west, and Man and Danané in the west. The western highlands also grow arabusta, a robusta-arabica hybrid cultivated since the 1960s and once marketed as "presidential coffee"; because it is slower to bear fruit, few farmers grow it.

Coffee trees flower several times a year, and the harvest mostly runs from August to January, largely by hand; most coffee is sun-dried (natural process). Green beans are graded from 0 to 2 according to defects, with grade 0 the highest. The industry also notes that quality has historically suffered from defects and inadequate post-harvest controls — precisely the key to adding value. In 2014 the government invested 8 billion CFA francs with a target of 400,000 tonnes a year by 2020, but actual output remained far below this; the US Department of Agriculture estimated exports of about 800,000 bags (60 kg each) in 2022/23.

## How Ivorians drink coffee

Côte d'Ivoire itself has no deep coffee-drinking tradition, and instant coffee is the most common. Black coffee sold at street stalls is a daily habit for many, while higher-income consumers prefer capsule coffee. In recent years, cafés focused on specialty coffee have also appeared in Abidjan, seeking to spread knowledge about origins and brewing.

## One line for Asian readers

Ivorian coffee mostly enters the instant and blend markets as robusta; if you are travelling or sourcing there, keep an eye out for the arabusta of the western highlands and the new specialty cafés.
