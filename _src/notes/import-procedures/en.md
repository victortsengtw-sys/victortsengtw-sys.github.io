If you sell goods into Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer), your local importer will complete almost every formality on GUCE. Understanding this process helps you prepare documents early and avoid having goods held up once they reach the port. What follows is an introduction; always check the latest guidance from the authorities and your customs broker for actual requirements.

## What is GUCE?

GUCE (Guichet Unique du Commerce Extérieur, the foreign trade single window) was set up in 2015 and celebrated its tenth anniversary in June 2025; it is run by a state-owned company. It brings import, export and transit formalities together on a single digital platform, connecting traders, government agencies, banks and technical partners. The US Department of Commerce's Country Commercial Guide notes that all import procedures go through GUCE, and that importers and the customs agents they appoint must register and submit documents on the platform; most importers use a licensed customs broker (commissionnaire en douane agréé).

## The import process in five steps

| Step | What it involves |
|---|---|
| 1. Obtain an import/export code | Apply to the Direction de la Régulation et des Échanges (DRE) for a Code Import/Export, with documents such as the trade register entry, tax registration and a tax clearance certificate |
| 2. Open a GUCE account | According to the government's public services portal, this requires identity documents for the legal representative and users, plus proof of a valid import/export code |
| 3. Submit the import declaration (FDI) | Complete the Fiche de Déclaration à l'Importation on GUCE; it must be approved before the goods arrive |
| 4. Value and classification inspection | Goods with an FOB value of 1 million CFA francs or more require a Final Classification and Valuation Report (RFCV); below this threshold, an inspection certificate (AV) is generally issued |
| 5. Customs declaration and payment | Handled by the Direction Générale des Douanes (DGD); goods are released once duties and taxes are paid |

According to the same guide, inspection-related fees are 0.75% of the FOB value (GUCE platform) plus 1.05% (inspection company), with lower rates for petroleum products and timber.

## Documents exporters need to prepare

A standard import usually requires: a commercial invoice in French (showing both parties' details, net and gross weight, CIF value, terms of sale and a detailed description of the goods), a certificate of origin, and a bill of lading or air waybill. A packing list is not legally required but is strongly recommended.

Some goods need additional documents: agricultural products, chemicals, pharmaceuticals and defence-related items require a prior import authorisation (API); food and agricultural products need sanitary and phytosanitary certificates; medicines and medical devices must be registered with the Ministry of Health; and telecommunications and electronic equipment may require type approval.

## How duties are calculated

Côte d'Ivoire applies the common external tariff of the West African Economic and Monetary Union (UEMOA). Customs duty is levied ad valorem on the CIF value of the goods, plus 18% VAT, a 1% statistical tax, the community solidarity levy and, for some goods, excise duties. Sensitive goods such as alcoholic beverages and petroleum products may be subject to additional charges or minimum import prices.

## One line for Asian companies

Before shipping, confirm with your local importer that the FDI has been approved and whether any prior authorisation is needed, and prepare your invoice and certificate of origin in French — that will spare you most customs headaches.
