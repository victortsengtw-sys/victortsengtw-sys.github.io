Companies preparing to enter Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer) often include a Hofstede cultural-dimensions chart in their slides, using the scores to show "what West African culture is like". The tool does have some value, but the sources and limits of the West African data are far more complicated than most presentations suggest. Before using it, be clear about what it can and cannot tell you.

## Where the scores come from: "West Africa" is really three countries

Hofstede's classic research was based on surveys of IBM employees. It first proposed four dimensions — power distance, individualism, masculinity and uncertainty avoidance — with further dimensions such as long-term orientation added later. In the frequently cited score tables, "West Africa" is a combined region made up of three countries: Ghana, Nigeria and Sierra Leone.

| Dimension | West Africa | Taiwan | Brief explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Power distance (PDI) | 77 | 58 | The extent to which members of a society accept an unequal distribution of power |
| Individualism (IDV) | 20 | 17 | The lower the score, the greater the emphasis on the group and close-knit in-groups |
| Masculinity (MAS) | 46 | 45 | Leaning towards competitive, assertive values or modest, caring ones |
| Uncertainty avoidance (UAI) | 54 | 69 | The degree of unease with ambiguous, unstructured situations |

The table shows that West Africa and Taiwan are quite close on individualism and masculinity; the main differences are West Africa's higher power distance and lower uncertainty avoidance.

## How it can be used

- **As a starting point for questions**: a high power-distance score is a reminder to pay attention to hierarchy and decision-making in an organisation — for instance, to confirm who has the final say — rather than to assume that is how your counterpart works.
- **To reflect on yourself**: the value of a comparison often lies in revealing your own team's assumptions, such as its tolerance for rules, procedures and uncertainty.
- **Together with other information**: the scores only become meaningful when combined with the experience of local colleagues, actual organisational structures and industry knowledge.

## How it should not be used

- **Côte d'Ivoire is not in this "West Africa" dataset.** The scores come from Ghana, Nigeria and Sierra Leone; applying them directly to Côte d'Ivoire means describing one country with its neighbours' data. When Gert Jan Hofstede launched an interactive map on the official Hofstede website in 2021, he cautioned that many countries' scores were merely his personal estimates and should not be treated as validated data; he also pointed out that in Africa, borders drawn in the colonial era are not necessarily cultural boundaries.
- **African countries differ widely.** A 2015 comparison of 15 sub-Saharan African countries by IESE Business School found the greatest variation on the masculinity dimension, and the differences among West African countries were the most pronounced.
- **It cannot describe individuals.** National averages cannot be used to draw conclusions about a particular manager or company — what scholars call the "ecological fallacy". Hofstede himself stressed that a culture is not "an individual writ large".
- **The data itself is contested.** Common criticisms include: samples drawn from employees of a single company, small numbers of respondents in some countries, equating nations with cultures, and data that is dated and treats culture as static. The official Hofstede website also reminds users that the dimensions are constructs to aid understanding, and that the scores should be treated with caution.

## One line for Asian companies

Treat Hofstede scores as a rough map, not a photograph of the destination; truly getting to know your Ivorian partners still takes conversation after conversation and time spent together.
