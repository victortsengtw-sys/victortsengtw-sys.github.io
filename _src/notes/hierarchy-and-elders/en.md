In Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer), respect for elders and superiors underpins everyday interaction, and it carries straight into the meeting room. For teams from Taiwan, Japan or Korea the underlying logic will feel familiar, but the way it is expressed differs in places, and it is worth understanding beforehand.

## What hierarchy rests on: age, position, experience

Several business-culture guides note that interaction in Côte d'Ivoire is strongly shaped by seniority, which combines age, position and experience. Common practices include:

- **Greet the most senior person first**: on entering the room, acknowledge the highest-ranking or eldest member of the other team first.
- **Use titles and surnames**: early exchanges are formal — Monsieur/Madame plus the surname, or the person's job title. Adjust once the relationship develops, following the other person's lead.
- **Let the senior person speak first**: a 2024 overview in *Forbes Afrique* advises letting the chief or most senior person speak before you do.
- **Offer your seat and listen**: giving up your seat to an elder, letting them speak first and listening attentively to the end is the most direct way to show respect.

## Levels of decision-making and communication

In many Ivorian organisations, decision-making power is concentrated at the very top, and information usually travels up and down the hierarchy. Two practical points follow:

1. **Make sure you are dealing with the right level**: a mid-level contact may need to seek approval from above, and patient waiting usually works better than repeated chasing.
2. **Do not go over people's heads**: bypassing your counterpart to approach their boss directly is easily seen as disrespectful. Likewise, *Forbes Afrique* warns against skipping local elders or intermediaries to go straight to the person in authority.

## How to disagree

Openly challenging a superior's decision in public is uncommon. Criticism and objections are often expressed indirectly — through examples, stories, proverbs or hints — although the younger generation tends to be more direct.

So:

| Situation | Better approach |
|---|---|
| The other side's senior manager makes a flawed proposal | Raise it privately after the meeting, or frame it as a question |
| The answer you receive is vague | Pay attention to tone, body language and silences, then find a chance to confirm |
| You need to express a different view | First acknowledge the other side's intention, then propose an alternative |

At the same time, be candid. *Forbes Afrique* notes that local partners will often withdraw quickly if they feel misled: being tactful is not the same as being evasive.

## Eye contact and body language

In some parts of Africa, young people deliberately avoid looking elders in the eye as a mark of respect; in the West this may be misread as evasiveness. If someone lowers their head slightly or avoids your gaze, it is usually politeness, not lack of interest. Conversely, when you are facing an elderly chief or elder, a humbler posture and a slower, calmer tone will be noticed.

## One line for Asian teams

Bring into the meeting room the same courtesy you would show a chairman or an elder in Asia: greet the most senior person first, let them speak first, and disagree in private — and you will have got most of it right.
