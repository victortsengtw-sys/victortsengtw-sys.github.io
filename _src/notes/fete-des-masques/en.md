The Man region in the west of Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer) is where many people first encounter West African mask culture. For local communities, a mask is not simply a handicraft but a presence that "makes an appearance", with an identity and duties of its own. If you are interested in African art, seeing a mask dance in a village with your own eyes will bring museum collections vividly to life.

## Where is Man?

Man lies in west-central Côte d'Ivoire, about 450 km from Abidjan, and is the main town of the mountainous Montagnes region, where the terrain is far more rugged than in most of the country. Around the town are the Dent de Man peak, regarded as a local guardian symbol, and Mount Tonkoui, the country's second-highest peak, about 15 km away. Lonely Planet notes that the area is famous for its village masks, and stilt dancers often perform at mask festivals.

## The role of masks in Dan society

The Man area is one of the main homelands of the Dan (also known as the Yacouba), who live across western Côte d'Ivoire and north-eastern Liberia. According to the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, the Dan give invisible forest spirits the form of masks; a mask is regarded as spiritually charged whether or not anyone is wearing it. A male performer able to dance a given mask is believed to have first been summoned in a dream by that mask's spirit.

Masks are also ranked, and are tied to local political and religious life. Some masks are entertainers, amusing villagers with dances, skits and mime; some are worn in races by the community's best runners; and revered masks may be elevated to represent a section of the village, or even serve as "judicial masks" that arbitrate disputes. A travel writer's observations in Dan villages also describe how drumbeats herald the masks' arrival and the whole village gathers in the square; male masks stride through on stilts, while female masks seem to glide across the sand, in performances that celebrate the harvest and protect the village.

## Mask festivals: varied in form, variable in timing

The "Fête des Masques of Man" is not a single event with a fixed date, but rather a combination of individual villages' own mask celebrations and festivals organised in recent years by local associations. Sources also disagree on timing: the French edition of Lonely Planet places it in February in villages around Man, while other sources say the end of the year.

To take a recent example, the fifth edition of Festi-San, the festival of masks and traditional rhythms of Dan culture, was held from 18 to 20 April 2025 in Sandougou-Soba, about 34 km from Man, with organisers expecting around 10,000 attendees. Besides mask parades, the programme included visits, a clean-up of the sacred forest, a marathon, a cooking contest and tree planting, with the Gué mask — central to the Dan cosmology — as the star.

## What to check before you go

Mask performances are usually linked to ritual, and some parts are open only to certain members of the community. Before setting off, confirm that year's dates through a local travel agency or the organisers; once there, ask your guide to check with the village elders before taking photos, approaching a mask or entering a sacred forest.

## One line for Asian readers

If you want to get to know West African masks, consider adding Man to your itinerary: the moment you see a mask drummed into a village square, you will better understand why these works are treated locally as "living beings".
