In Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer), forms of address matter a great deal on formal occasions. Acknowledging each distinguished guest at the start of a speech, or opening a letter with the right title, is seen as a mark of respect; conversely, calling a lawyer "Monsieur" or addressing a minister by name may suggest you do not know the rules. For the abbreviated company job titles found on business cards, see "Reading a French business card"; this article focuses on the forms of address used for people.

## Common forms of address at a glance

| Form of address | For | Usage tips |
|---|---|---|
| Monsieur / Madame | Any adult | The most basic form; in formal letters, write it in full with a capital initial |
| Monsieur le Ministre / Monsieur le Directeur | Holders of a specific office | "Monsieur/Madame + le/la + title", used face to face and at the start of letters |
| Maître (abbreviated Me) | Lawyers, notaries | e.g. *Maître Koné*; a social convention rather than a title laid down by law |
| Docteur (abbreviated Dr) | Physicians | Also *Monsieur le docteur*, *Madame la docteure* |
| Excellence | Ambassadors, some heads of state and senior officials | *Votre Excellence* face to face, *Son Excellence* when referring to them |
| Honorable | Members of parliament | Ivorian media often place *Honorable* before an MP's name |

## Common pitfalls

**More Excellence is not better.** The Académie française defines Excellence as a title of protocol that may be used for heads of state or government, ministers, ambassadors and bishops, among others — *Votre Excellence* when addressing them, *Son Excellence* when referring to them. In practice, however, usage varies from country to country. A writer specialising in diplomatic protocol argued in a 2017 article that the title is essentially diplomatic language, used mainly for ambassadors; countries such as France, the United States and China do not use it for their heads of state or cabinet members, and on domestic occasions the job title is preferable, e.g. *Monsieur le Premier ministre*. He also observed that in recent years Excellence has been overused at some events in West Africa. For foreign guests, the safe approach is: Excellence for ambassadors, *Monsieur/Madame le/la Ministre* for the host country's ministers, and follow how the organiser's programme or seating plan writes it.

**Maître is not only for barristers.** Under Quebec's guidelines, Maître is reserved for practising lawyers and notaries. A 2025 report from Gabon stressed that calling lawyers Maître stems from historical custom and is not prescribed by the relevant regulations. When you deal with a notaire (notary) or an avocat (lawyer) in a transaction, Maître is a safe choice for either.

**Docteur depends on context.** According to the Académie française dictionary, docteur refers both to someone holding the highest university degree and to a physician. As a form of address, Quebec's guidelines reserve Docteur mainly for physicians, dentists and veterinarians; practice regarding "Dr" for doctoral degree holders varies between countries and institutions, so the best approach is to address people as their business card or signature does.

**Traditional titles.** In obituary listings published in Ivorian newspapers, alongside Monsieur, Madame and Honorable you will also see traditional titles such as *Nanan*, placed next to the names of village or traditional chiefs. These titles vary by ethnic group and region; if you need to address a traditional chief on a formal occasion, it is best to ask the organiser or local colleagues first.

## One line for Asian teams

When in doubt, "Monsieur/Madame + title" is almost never wrong; then fine-tune it to match the business card, the programme and the organiser's own usage — that is the most fitting way to address people.
