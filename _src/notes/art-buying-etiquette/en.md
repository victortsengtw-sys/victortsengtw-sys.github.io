In Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer), from Abidjan's craft markets to workshops across the country, you can buy wood carvings, masks, hand-woven textiles and Korhogo cloth. For Asian buyers, the first thing to think through is not "is it cheap?" but: what kind of object is this? Can it legally leave the country? And is the way I am buying it respectful of the maker and of the culture itself?

## First, tell them apart: contemporary craft or antique?

Under Côte d'Ivoire's 1987 cultural heritage protection law (Loi n° 87-806), the rules on taking objects out of the country depend mainly on the nature of the object:

| Category | Export rules (per the text of the law) |
|---|---|
| Movable property listed as national cultural heritage | Export prohibited in principle; temporary export allowed only in exceptional cases, and the object must be returned |
| Works of art and antiques (whether listed or not) | Private exporters must obtain prior authorisation, issued as an "export certificate"; the State may exercise a right of pre-emption under the rules |
| Modern art and contemporary crafts | Not subject to the export certificate requirement above |

The law also provides that anyone who exports or attempts to export listed objects may have them seized and confiscated, to be added to the national collections.

In other words, a newly painted Korhogo cloth or a freshly made workshop carving is in a completely different legal position from a mask claimed to be "a hundred years old". If a seller insists that an item is old, or a genuine piece used in rituals, be aware that it may fall within the scope of the export certificate requirement.

## Documents and institutions: who to check with

The German federal government's cultural property protection portal notes that Côte d'Ivoire is a party to the 1970 UNESCO Convention on the Means of Prohibiting and Preventing the Illicit Import, Export and Transfer of Ownership of Cultural Property, and has also joined the 1995 UNIDROIT Convention on Stolen or Illegally Exported Cultural Objects. The institutions it lists in connection with export permits include the Musée des Civilisations de Côte d'Ivoire in Abidjan and the National Costume Museum in Grand-Bassam. The site also cautions that its information may be incomplete or out of date.

In addition, in 2023 the Ivorian government put forward a bill to amend the 1987 law, aimed at strengthening protection, increasing penalties and aligning with international standards. In 2025 it also signed a cooperation agreement with Switzerland on the import, export and return of cultural property, and in April 2026 adopted a decree ratifying that agreement. The rules are still evolving, so before buying expensive items or anything that may be an antique, the safest course is to check the latest procedures directly with the cultural heritage department of the Ministry of Culture or the museums mentioned above.

## Authentication and bargaining: a few practical principles

The following are principles you can follow when buying, not local legal rules:

- **Ask about provenance, not just age**: the International Council of Museums (ICOM) Red List for West Africa advises buyers to check provenance and legal documents, and not to buy if there is any doubt about legality.
- **Appreciate the value of new work**: contemporary craft does not need to pass itself off as antique to have value; a new piece whose maker or workshop can be named is often more worth collecting than an "old piece" of unknown origin.
- **Bargain politely**: express your appreciation of the work first, then ask the price; make a reasonable offer in a calm tone, and if you cannot agree, simply thank the seller and leave courteously.
- **Keep records**: ask for a receipt and note the seller, place and a description of the piece — useful when leaving the country or reselling later.
- **Check the rules at your destination**: find out in advance whether your own country has declaration or quarantine requirements on entry (for wooden items, for example).

## One line for Asian readers

When buying art in Côte d'Ivoire, the best collecting habit is "ask first, then take it home": proper documents and a clear provenance are what give a work its true worth.
