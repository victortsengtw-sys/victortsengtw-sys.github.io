When researching West African markets, you often come across two names at once: UEMOA and ECOWAS (CEDEAO in French). Both are regional economic organisations with partly overlapping membership, but they differ in size, function and history. Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer) belongs to both, so anyone trading there will run into both sets of rules.

## The two organisations at a glance

| | UEMOA (West African Economic and Monetary Union) | ECOWAS / CEDEAO (Economic Community of West African States) |
|---|---|---|
| Founded | 10 January 1994 in Dakar, Senegal | Treaty of Lagos signed on 28 May 1975; revised treaty in 1993 |
| Headquarters | Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso | Abuja, Nigeria |
| Members | 8 countries: Benin, Burkina Faso, Côte d'Ivoire, Guinea-Bissau, Mali, Niger, Senegal, Togo | 12 countries since January 2025: Benin, Cabo Verde, Côte d'Ivoire, The Gambia, Ghana, Guinea, Guinea-Bissau, Liberia, Nigeria, Senegal, Sierra Leone, Togo |
| Common currency | Yes: members share the West African CFA franc, with monetary policy set by the Central Bank of West African States (BCEAO) | No: each country uses its own currency |

## The difference: deep integration vs broad integration

UEMOA's defining feature is that it is "economic plus monetary". Its treaty objectives include creating a common market with free movement of people, goods, services and capital; bringing members' macroeconomic performance into line through multilateral surveillance; coordinating sectoral policies in areas such as agriculture, transport, energy and telecommunications; and, where necessary, harmonising national legislation, especially tax rules. With a shared currency and central bank, its integration runs deeper.

ECOWAS is broader in scope: beyond French-speaking countries, it also includes English-speaking ones such as Nigeria and Ghana. Its long-term goal is to build an economic and monetary union in stages, but it currently has no common currency.

## Where they overlap: the common external tariff

For importers and exporters, the most practical point of overlap is customs duties. The ECOWAS common external tariff (TEC in French) was originally based on UEMOA's four-band structure. On 25 October 2013 it was expanded to five bands, and from 2015 it replaced UEMOA's own common external tariff:

| Rate | Category |
|---|---|
| 0% | Essential social goods |
| 5% | Basic necessities, raw materials, capital goods |
| 10% | Intermediate goods and inputs |
| 20% | Final consumer goods |
| 35% | Specific goods designated for economic development purposes |

There are also supporting instruments such as safeguard, anti-dumping and countervailing measures. Which band a given product falls into should be checked against the tariff schedule published by customs.

Intra-regional trade remains modest: citing UEMOA data, the French Ministry of the Economy's economic service in Abidjan notes that in 2018 intra-UEMOA trade accounted for 16.1% of member states' total trade, with petroleum products making up more than 40% of it.

## What changed in 2025

The withdrawal of Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger from ECOWAS formally took effect on 29 January 2025; the three countries have formed their own Alliance of Sahel States (AES). ECOWAS announced at the time that, pending a decision by heads of state on future relations, existing regional trade arrangements would remain in place for now, and that passports and identity documents bearing the ECOWAS logo issued by the three countries would continue to be recognised.

According to reports in early 2025, the three countries remain UEMOA members, and goods continue to move normally among the eight. In July 2025, however, representatives of the three walked out of a UEMOA ministerial meeting over a dispute about the rotating presidency, showing that relations remain uncertain. If your business involves these three countries, check the latest arrangements regularly.

## One line for Asian companies

Think of UEMOA as "the eight-country inner circle with a shared currency" and ECOWAS as "the wider customs and trade framework". When planning a regional strategy from Côte d'Ivoire, keep both maps in view.
