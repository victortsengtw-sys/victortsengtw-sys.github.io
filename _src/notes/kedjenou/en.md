In Côte d'Ivoire (in West Africa, French-speaking, the world's largest cocoa producer), if you could order only one "home-style stew", many people would recommend kedjenou. It looks like nothing more than chicken stewed with tomato and onion, but what really matters is the method: a sealed pot, very little water, low heat, and a clay vessel. Understanding this dish is also a way into the logic of many traditional West African kitchens.

## A chicken stew from the centre of the country

Kedjenou is generally thought to come from the Baoulé, an Akan people who migrated around the 18th century from what is now Ghana into central Côte d'Ivoire. One common explanation holds that the name kedjenou means "to shake gently" in Baoulé, referring precisely to the way the pot is shaken during cooking rather than opened and stirred.

Traditionally, cooks prefer free-range village chickens or guinea fowl, whose meat is leaner and firmer, cut into bone-in pieces. The most common version today uses chicken or guinea fowl with tomato, onion, garlic, chilli, ginger and bay leaves. Some sources note that early versions used game, with poultry becoming the norm later. In maquis (Côte d'Ivoire's affordable open-air eateries), kedjenou may also be made with fish or other meats, so it is worth asking when you order.

## The canari: a clay pot that lets ingredients make their own juices

Traditional kedjenou is cooked in a clay pot called a canari. The bone-in chicken pieces and vegetables go into the pot, which is sealed with banana leaves or similar, then set over a wood fire or buried in hot embers to cook slowly.

The key is that almost no water is added, and little or no oil. All the liquid comes from the moisture and steam released by the meat and vegetables themselves, so the meat stays tender and the flavour more concentrated. The lid stays on throughout cooking; instead, every so often the cook picks up the pot and shakes it gently to turn the ingredients and stop them sticking.

The canari is a craft in its own right. In the village of Motiamo, near Bondoukou in north-eastern Côte d'Ivoire, women have made pottery for generations: they take clay and limestone from the surrounding area and knead them into a paste, shape the pots by hand, fire them on the ground over wood since there is no kiln, and finally dip them in a specially prepared liquid to colour them.

## Cooking and eating it in a modern kitchen

City households do not always have a clay pot or charcoal fire, so they often use a heavy-bottomed pot, a lidded casserole or even a pressure cooker, while keeping to the same principles: little water, lid on, and an occasional shake.

| Common accompaniment | Notes |
|---|---|
| attiéké | Fine-grained staple made from fermented cassava; the classic pairing |
| White rice | Very common in cities |
| Boiled yam or plantain | More common in the countryside |
| foufou | Cassava or yam pounded into a dough |

Chillies are often added whole rather than chopped, so their heat is released slowly; some cooks also add aubergine, sweet pepper or a little palm oil. In Ivorian diaspora communities in France and elsewhere, kedjenou is likewise a familiar home-cooked dish.

If you want to try making it yourself, the secret lies not in the proportions of spices but in "seal the pot, keep the heat low, and don't lift the lid to stir".

## One line for Asian readers

Kedjenou follows much the same logic as Asian clay-pot braised chicken: earthenware holds the heat and the ingredients' own juices carry the flavour. Make it your first dish for understanding West African home cooking, and you will get closer to the local kitchen than by starting with grilled food.
